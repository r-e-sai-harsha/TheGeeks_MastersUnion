Every 3 months, a person with diabetes in India gets one number: their HbA1c. In between, there are about 270 meals, and the doctor sees none of them. This is Chaya. Chaya means shadow. It is a digital twin that walks 2 hours ahead of a patient, warns the care team before a glucose spike or a low, and puts the doctor and the patient on the same page.

We are The Geeks, from Masters' Union: Sanskriti Saraf, Muskan Sharma and Sai Harsha Sadhu. This is our Phase 1 submission for the Happiest Health Digital Twin Challenge. In this video we will show you the problem, how the twin works, how well it performs, and the working product.

India has about 101 million people living with diabetes, and another 136 million with prediabetes. Indian diets get roughly 62% of their calories from carbohydrates, so the real damage happens after meals, as glucose spikes, several times a day. Yet care is built around a single quarterly lab test. A doctor sees one HbA1c value, adjusts a prescription, and waits 3 months. Between those visits, the spikes are invisible.

And India needs its own model. South Asians develop insulin resistance at a lower body weight, so Western risk scores under-call Indian patients. The plate is different too: rice, roti, dosa, biryani and sweets, each with its own load. And daily life matters: late dinners, short sleep, festival days and missed doses. So our use case is precise. We predict a glucose spike above 180 mg/dL, 2 hours before it happens, for adults with Type 2 diabetes or prediabetes.

Here is what a digital twin means in Chaya. Every patient gets a small physiological model of their own body: food enters the gut, glucose enters the blood, and insulin pushes it back down. 4 personal numbers shape it: insulin sensitivity, how strongly the body responds to insulin; beta-cell response, how much insulin the pancreas releases; basal glucose, where the patient settles with no food; and meal absorption, with the morning dawn effect. The twin starts from the health record, the static stream, and is tuned on the first 7 days of wearables, the dynamic stream. That is data fusion at the level of physiology. It also spots meals the patient forgot to log, and accounts for short sleep and missed metformin.

Because this challenge runs in a sandbox, all of our data is synthetic. We built our own generator, because we needed Indian meals, Indian prescriptions like teneligliptin and glimepiride, late dinners and festival days, and glucose physics that stays consistent with the HbA1c in each record. The cohort has 150 patients, 14 days each: 604,800 5-minute readings. It is realistically messy: patients log only some of their meals, under-estimate carbohydrates, miss doses and sleep badly, and the sensor has lag, noise and dropouts. Every health record is a FHIR R4 bundle with LOINC and SNOMED codes, so the same pipeline can later read real, consented records through ABDM.

This is the architecture, left to right. The health record and the wearables flow into an ingest layer, then the twin core: a prior from the record, calibration on the first week, an observer, a habit model and a forecaster. We fuse 18 record features, 25 wearable features and 11 from the twin. LightGBM predicts the spike and forecasts glucose 2 hours ahead, the same twin forecast read downwards warns of lows, and SHAP gives the reasons. The output goes to a simulated clinic and to a patient app. Page 2 shows the care loop, from warning to the patient reading the advice, and the deployment on Vercel in Mumbai over Supabase.

First, does the twin actually learn the patient? Because the data is synthetic, we know each person's true hidden physiology, so we can check. After 1 week of wearable data, the twin recovers basal glucose with a correlation of 0.99, insulin sensitivity at 0.84, and beta-cell response at 0.91. The health record alone reaches only 0.77, 0.75 and 0.88. The wearable stream is what makes the twin personal.

Second, does it predict spikes? We tested on 45 patients the model never saw in training. The area under the ROC curve is 0.942. Sensitivity is 83%, precision is 79%, and 87% of spikes are flagged at least an hour ahead. In plain words: 8 in 10 spikes, warned before they happen. The 2-hour glucose forecast has an error of about 24 mg/dL, and 99.3% of forecasts land in the clinically safe zones of the Clarke error grid.

Each stream adds signal. The health record alone scores 0.876. Wearables alone score 0.936. The physics twin alone, with no training at all, already scores 0.819. Fusing all of them gives the best result, 0.942.

So why build a twin if machine learning gets close on its own? Because real clinics start with very little data. With only 5 training patients, adding the twin lifts the score from 0.873 to 0.893. The physics carries knowledge the data has not taught the model yet. And the twin can answer what-if questions, which a black-box classifier cannot. Let us show you.

Every alert comes with its reasons. SHAP breaks each score into contributions, grouped into drivers a clinician recognises: the twin's forecast, a recent meal, activity, glucose trend, sleep, medication and meals the patient forgot to log. The twin's own forecast is the biggest single driver, so the physics is doing real work inside the model.

Now, from the guide menu, let us walk through Sanjay Krishnan's case. It is Sunday, 12:15, and Sanjay is about to eat lunch. He is 53, has had Type 2 diabetes for about 4 years, and takes metformin and glimepiride. His glucose is calm and in range. Over his first week, his twin learned that his insulin sensitivity is low, lower than his health record suggested. That is why lunch hits him hard. If he eats chicken biryani, the twin projects a peak of 237, with about 2 hours above 180.

Swap the rice for ragi mudde, and the peak drops to 194. Add a 20-minute walk after lunch, and the peak falls to 178, with 0 minutes above 180. That is advice a patient can actually follow, with a number attached for his own body.

Now, what actually happened. At 12:45, Sanjay logged an 81 g lunch. Chaya stamped his slip spike likely, 92%, and listed the reasons, 40 minutes before his glucose actually crossed 180. That is the window a care coordinator needs to make a call. Finally, the doctor chooses save and send to patient. The advice is written to the database with the twin's projection, and a note pops up with a link: open Sanjay's advice.

And here is exactly what Sanjay sees on his phone. The advice arrives in his doctor's handwriting, with what it should do: a peak of about 178 instead of 194. He taps got it, and the clinic sees that he read it. Today shows his glucose with the same violet shadow, plain warnings, and his medicines at his own usual dose times. The plus button logs a meal in 2 taps, and it lands on the clinic's trace. One tap switches to Hindi. He never sees risk scores or other patients.

Back in the clinic. Every patient now has a full name, so no two people in the register look alike. Sort by lowest time in range, or by low-glucose risk, and open another patient. Press replay, and watch the warnings appear before the spikes, not after them.

Open the health record, and it now says what kind of number you are looking at. A dot means a single reading from the record; a violet line means a trend from this week's sensor. Sanjay's lab HbA1c of 6.4 looks fine, but the sensor's estimate this week is 6.9, and his waking glucose was above 130 on 6 of 7 mornings. Every other value has a target bar and a plain word: high, low, raised or at target.

Every warning can be marked as reviewed, which keeps an audit trail. Hover any small i button and a tooltip explains that panel in plain words, and a Sources button shows exactly where each number comes from.

Spikes are only half of the safety story. The same twin forecast, read downwards, warns of lows. Here is Geeta Mukherjee, on glimepiride and basal insulin, on Monday morning. Her glucose is 107 and falling, and the twin forecasts a dip to about 73. The stamp reads low likely, in a deep blue, with the medication listed as a reason. On her phone, the low card moves to the very top. On the held-out week, Chaya warned of 7 of 10 lows, a median of 80 minutes early, at about 1 low warning per patient a week.

A warning only matters if someone acts on it. Under who acts on this, the doctor picks an owner, the care coordinator, the doctor on call or the diabetes educator, and a contact-by time. The owner can open a prefilled WhatsApp message, or log a call and its outcome. If nobody acts in time, the case goes overdue and moves to the doctor on call. Every step is stored.

The Rx pad opens from the Advise / Prescribe button in the bottom-right corner, like an email draft, and it can be minimised or closed. Set metformin to missed, and last night's sleep to 5 hours. The twin redraws the next 4 hours, and the peak rises. This is a conversation a doctor can have with a patient, using numbers for this patient's body rather than generic advice.

This is what a hospital buyer would look at: clinic outcomes. Patients meeting the time-in-range goal, daily time in range, low-glucose safety, alert load, and a funnel showing whether warnings turned into action, from reviewed to seen by the patient. Back in the clinic, the alerts control sets how readily spike warnings fire: about 2.6 to 4.6 warnings per patient a day, for 74% to 98% of spikes warned.

Every number has a source. The data sources page lists all 25 parameters in 4 groups, each with its use, where the prototype gets it, where a clinic would, its standard code and how often it arrives.

And the guide now includes a patient app tour, 11 short steps for anyone opening the app cold.

Under the hood is a real backend. The health endpoint reports the database and its 45 patients; reviews, escalations, advice, read receipts and patient logs live in Supabase Postgres with row-level security. Everything is open source under the MIT licence.

Chaya is built to support the doctor, not to replace them. It is decision support only, with a clinician in the loop and no dosing advice. Alerts are merged into episodes, and the clinic sets the threshold, seeing the trade-off as it does. Lows come first on the patient's screen. There is no real patient data in this prototype. When it moves to real records, consent will flow through ABDM, in line with India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, and the system is FHIR-native from day one.

Today, the prototype covers the clinic and a bilingual patient app, for both spikes and lows, live on Vercel and Supabase. Next, we validate on public glucose datasets such as CGMacros and BIG IDEAs. Then a supervised pilot with a partner diabetes clinic. And then we extend: the WhatsApp Business API for escalations, ABDM-consented records, and a hypertension twin.

Diabetes care in India does not have to be reactive. With Chaya, we see the spike before it happens, we make sure someone acts on it, and we show the patient how to avoid it. The live app and all our code are linked below. Thank you from The Geeks.
